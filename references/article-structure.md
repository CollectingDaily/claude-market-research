# The article, section by section

Dan's section list is in `structure.md`. This file is what goes inside each one: its job, its length, what it needs before it can be written, and how it fails.

The order is Dan's and does not change. The report runs roughly **3,000 to 4,500 words** across eleven sections. It is a document someone gives an email address for, so it has to be worth more than the address.

**One governing rule.** The reader is new to the field, excited, and might want to make money later. Every section is written to someone who knows the subject from the outside and nothing from the inside. Assume they know the films, the players or the characters. Assume they know nothing about grading, provenance, premiums or how an auction works.

---

## 1. The lowdown

**Job:** tell them what this market is and that they are not alone in it.

**Length:** 250 to 400 words.

Open on the field, not on money. One paragraph on what people collect and why it holds anyone's attention. Then demographics: how many collectors, where they are, age range, gender split.

**Needs research.** Demographics rarely sit in an auction record, so the sources are trade bodies, grading companies' published volumes, platform data and market reports. A grading company's own annual volume figures are a real number; a market-size estimate from an SEO content mill is not. Where a genuine number does not exist, say which part is not documented rather than reaching for one.

**Fails when** it opens with an auction record. That is section 6's job, and leading with it makes the reader feel poor on page one.

---

## 2. Trends

**Job:** show the market moving, with prices attached.

**Length:** 600 to 900 words, the longest section in the report.

Three parts, in Dan's order.

**What is popular.** Props, clothing, autographs, sealed material, graded cards, whatever the field's categories are. Say why, and put a price against it. Dan's example is the mechanism to follow: Harry Potter is rising because the audience that grew up with the films now has a salary. That is a demographic fact producing a price, and it is the shape every trend paragraph should take.

**Who is popular.** Names, with the reason and the figure.

**Who and what is falling out of favour.** This is the section that makes the report credible. Anyone can list things going up. A report that names what is cooling reads as honest, and the reader trusts the rest of it. Say it plainly and source it as hard as the rises.

**Auction price examples throughout**, as Dan asks. Not gathered into a block at the end.

**Fails when** every trend points up, or when a trend is asserted without a figure.

---

## 3. Getting started

**Job:** the practical core. For this reader it is the most useful part of the report.

**Length:** 900 to 1,200 words across six subsections.

**Glossary.** Field-specific and short. Movies: screen-used, screen-matched, production-made, replica, hero prop, stunt prop, COA. Cards: the grading scales, population reports, slabs, 1st edition, shadowless, error cards. Comics: slabbing, restoration, signature series, key issues, census. Define by the difference that changes the price, not by dictionary definition. *Screen-used* means it was on set; *screen-matched* means someone can point at the frame. The second is worth multiples of the first, and that is the sentence worth writing.

**How to spot a fake.** What to physically check, and who the reputable authenticators are, named. Research it online from the graders, the houses and the authenticators themselves; our own articles are never read (SKILL.md, 13 October 2026).

**Where to buy safely.** Auction houses, dealers, eBay and private sellers, with the trade-offs of each and the red flags. Honest about the risks of each channel, including the ones we compete with.

**Budget entry points.** What is realistically collectible at, say, £100, £1,000 and £10,000. **This is the section that converts.** A report where everything costs six figures tells the reader they are not welcome. Name real things at real prices.

**Care, storage and display.** Light, humidity, framing, handling. Short and concrete.

**Common beginner mistakes.** Overpaying, poor provenance, buying reproductions unknowingly. Written as what to do instead, not as a scolding.

**Fails when** it turns into a sales pitch for buying from us. The section earns trust by being useful first.

---

## 4. Upcoming market catalysts

**Job:** give them a reason this year matters.

**Length:** 200 to 350 words.

Anniversaries, film and game releases, studio or estate sales, museum shows, rule changes at the grading companies. Say what the catalyst is, when it lands, and what it plausibly does to demand.

**Check the arithmetic against the release year.** Dan's example was the 100th anniversary of *The Wizard of Oz*: the film is 1939, so that is 2039. *Star Wars* turns fifty in 2027, which is a real near-term catalyst and one our own 1977 Topps article already notes.

**Fails when** a catalyst is years away, or when the effect on demand is asserted with nothing behind it.

---

## 5. The top 10 most collectible [names in the field]

**Job:** a ranking the reader can argue with, which is what makes it shareable.

**Length:** 400 to 600 words, roughly 50 per entry.

Movie stars for film. Players or franchises for cards. Characters, sets or artists for Pokémon. Creators or characters for comics.

Each entry needs a reason for the ranking that is not just fame: how much material exists, how much of it is authenticated, what it does at auction, whether the estate releases anything. **Rank on collectability, not on celebrity.** A star with nothing in circulation is not collectible however famous.

**Fails when** it is a fame list. The reader can write that themselves.

---

## 6. The top 10 most expensive [items] ever sold

**Job:** the spectacle. It is what gets screenshotted.

**Length:** 400 to 600 words.

Price, item, house, date, one line on why it made that figure. Every one verified to the standard in `verification.md`, with the sale route named where it was not a public auction: a private sale, a brokered deal or a fractional stake is not the same as a hammer price, and saying so is what separates our list from every other one online.

Our Collecting Daily articles have rankings in several fields. They are never read, not even for leads (SKILL.md, 13 October 2026): build the ranking from the houses' own results, and run it up to the report date, because a list that is a few months old misses every sale since.

**Fails when** hammer prices and all-in prices sit in the same column without saying which is which.

---

## 7. The expert view

**Job:** put a person in the document.

**Length:** 150 to 250 words.

A ghostwritten quote from Paul. Written as speech: contractions, first person, an opinion he would actually hold. It should say something the rest of the report cannot, because it comes from forty-five years in the trade rather than from a source.

**Fails when** it is corporate throat-clearing, or when it makes a claim that would need a footnote. An opinion needs no source; a figure does.

---

## 8. Investing

**Job:** the case for money, made late and made honestly.

**Length:** 400 to 600 words.

Price indices where a real one exists for the field. Examples of pieces that have sold twice, which is the cleanest possible evidence because it is the same object at two dates.

Then why invest: the structural arguments, low correlation, limited supply, a growing collector base.

**No index exists for every field.** Where none does, say so and use the record progression instead: what the category record was in 2011 against what it is now, both sourced. Do not build an index out of nothing.

**Fails when** it promises returns, or when a rise is shown without the risk. The reader is new and this section is the one that can cause real harm.

---

## 9. Stock for sale

**Job:** turn interest into an enquiry.

**Length:** 200 to 400 words plus the pieces.

Pulled live from the relevant PFC collection, not written from memory. **A spread of prices**, not just the top end, because section 3 has just told them what is collectible at £1,000 and this section has to prove it.

Each piece: what it is, why it is interesting, the price. Stock quantity governs any scarcity claim, and a term the listing does not state does not appear.

**Where PFC holds no stock in the field, omit this section.** Do not substitute a category we do stock, and do not write about valuations or grading advice instead. Renumber what follows.

---

## 10. Why buy from Paul Fraser Collectibles

**Job:** credibility, briefly.

**Include only if PFC sells in the field or has real expertise in it.** With no stock and no expertise (trading cards and Pokémon), omit it and renumber.

**Length:** 200 to 300 words.

Forty-five years, the Lifetime Moneyback Guarantee of Authenticity, institutions sourced for, the coverage. Facts, not adjectives. The company brochure in `claude-workspace/Lead Market` is the source for this and it can be summarised rather than rewritten.

---

## 11. Have a question?

**Job:** one action.

**Length:** 50 to 100 words.

One call to action, phone and email, nothing competing with it. Dan's spec says "Book a call", but there is no booking link on PFC's site, so a report that says "book" sends the reader nowhere. Write "Call or email us: the details are below" and put the contact box under it, unless a booking link is supplied. Never add "speak to someone who handles this material every day" to a field where we have neither stock nor expertise.

---

## Sources

**Not a section of the article.** Every source the report uses goes in the `Sources` field of the frontmatter, and nothing is cited in the body: no footnote markers, no inline links, no Sources heading at the foot.

The reader gets clean prose. The record of where each figure came from sits in the frontmatter, where an editor can check it against the evidence log before the report goes out.

One line per source in that field: the page title and the URL. Every one opened and read in this session.
