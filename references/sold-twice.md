# Sold-twice examples: the same lot, or it is not an example

Section 8 asks for pieces that have sold twice. These are the most persuasive
figures in the whole report, because a world record tells a reader what the best
object on earth is worth while a resale tells them what an ordinary good piece did
over a decade. They are also the easiest thing in the report to get wrong.

## What happened in September 2026

Eight pairs were supplied for the movie memorabilia report, sourced from ChatGPT.
Checked against the auction records, **six were wrong**:

| Claim | Defect |
| --- | --- |
| Darth Vader helmet, three legs | The *Empire* helmet sold publicly **once**: $1,152,000, Profiles in History, 27 Sept 2019. The 2003 sale was **Bob Anderson's** helmet, $115,000. Two legs did not exist. |
| Ruby slippers, three legs | **Three different pairs** chained as one. The 2012 leg was not a sale: the Academy acquired a pair for an **undisclosed** price. |
| Luke Skywalker lightsaber | 2008 was a **different** lightsaber at $200,600, not $240,000. A Luke lightsaber was also **withdrawn from a 2018 sale over authenticity**. |
| Mjolnir | 2012 was a **stunt** prop, est. $4,000-6,000. 2017 was a **hero** prop, est. £20,000-30,000. Different objects. |
| Buddy the Elf costume | "2003" is the year the film was **made**. "£28,000" matches Propstore's **estimate**, not a result. |
| Superman costume | £312,500 in 2022 is real, but **two different Reeve costumes** sold around then. |

Only the **Casino Royale poker table** survived intact, and the **Dr No bathrobe**
half survived: same robe confirmed, 2001 price unverifiable.

The pattern is consistent and worth recognising on sight: **a real, checkable recent
result bolted onto an earlier sale of a different object.** The recent figure is what
makes it convincing. The earlier leg is where it fails. This is what a language model
produces when asked for a table it cannot source.

## The standard

A pair goes in the report only when all four hold.

1. **The same lot, stated by a house, not inferred by you.** Propstore's catalogue
   entry for the poker table reads: "This lot was previously sold in the 50 Years of
   James Bond - The Auction held by Christies in Sept-Oct 2012." That sentence is what
   makes it usable. A matching description is not enough: two Christopher Reeve
   Superman costumes, two Vader helmets and two Mjolnirs all sold within a few years
   of each other.
2. **Both legs fetched and read.** Not a search summary. Not a listicle. The house's
   own result, or a published sale report that names the lot.
3. **Both legs are sales.** Not an estimate, not a production year, not a museum
   acquisition at an undisclosed price, not a fractional stake.
4. **One currency.** Both legs in the currency the lot actually sold in, so the person
   writing can state the percentage in it and say so. Converting two legs at two
   different dates destroys the figure.

## Where to find real ones

Auction house provenance lines are the only reliable seam. Propstore, Christie's,
Bonhams and Heritage all state "previously sold in..." when they know it, because it
supports their own estimate. Search the house's catalogue text for that phrasing
rather than searching for a return figure.

Two verified pairs are worth more than eight that collapse the first time a client
searches one of them. If only one survives, run one.

## Rejected pairs: in the research document

The movie memorabilia report carried a short paragraph after the table warning the
reader that pairs like these circulate with the second sale attached to an earlier
sale of a different object. It is no longer printed: a market report shows the
reader no doubt and no checking (the user's decision, 22 October 2026). The
standard above is the protection, and every printed pair meets it. The research
document lists each pair rejected under it, beneath section 8's table, with the
condition it fails, so the person writing does not reach for it and a client who
meets one of the bad chains elsewhere can be answered.
